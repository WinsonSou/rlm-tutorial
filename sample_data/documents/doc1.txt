Title: The Discovery of Penicillin

Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928 at St. Mary's Hospital in London. He noticed that a mold called Penicillium notatum had contaminated one of his petri dishes and was killing the surrounding bacteria. This accidental discovery would go on to revolutionize medicine.

However, Fleming lacked the resources to purify penicillin for clinical use. It wasn't until 1940 that Howard Florey and Ernst Boris Chain at Oxford University developed methods to mass-produce penicillin. Their work was driven by the urgent need for antibiotics during World War II.

The first patient treated with penicillin was Albert Alexander, a policeman who had developed a life-threatening infection from a scratch. While the initial treatment showed dramatic improvement, supplies ran out before he could be fully cured, and he ultimately died.

By 1944, penicillin was being mass-produced and was widely available to Allied forces. It saved countless lives during D-Day and subsequent operations. Fleming, Florey, and Chain shared the 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their contributions.

KEY_DETAIL: Fleming warned as early as 1945 that overuse of antibiotics could lead to resistant bacteria, a prediction that has proven devastatingly accurate in the modern era of superbugs like MRSA.
